The Even-Decade Theory: an autopsy

I believed the GOATs of every field are born in even decades. I tested it properly. It died twice, came back once, and taught me more than being right would have.

Sakshyam Patro · July 2026 · every number reproducible with one command · code & data

The verdict

Being born in an even decade multiplies your odds of all-time greatness by 0.99 (95% CI 0.87–1.12). Nothing. Null in four datasets, all six fields, every half-century since 1700, every fame threshold, and at the GOAT tier itself: the #1 of each of 93 fields splits 49 even, 44 odd. A coin flip.

Act I · The hunchEveryone I could name was even-decade

Jordan, 1963. LeBron, 1984. Bolt, 1986. Messi, 1987. Pelé, 1940. Napoleon, 1769. Newton, 1643. Call a decade even when its tens digit is even: the 40s, the 60s, the 80s. Every GOAT I could summon lived there, and the pattern seemed to sharpen as I climbed from top-10 lists toward the single greatest of each field.

Type a year. 1987 → the 80s → even decade, same as Messi and LeBron.

The problem: roughly half of everyone is born in an even decade. Seven remembered hits is exactly what a memory that keeps confirmations and drops misses would produce from pure noise. So instead of collecting more examples, I locked the rules first.

Before any dataThe rules, written down in advance

Everything gameable was fixed in a pre-registration before a single birth year was downloaded:

The bet. Group A = born in an even decade. Eligibility window: births 1700–1989 (older records are unreliable in a way that turns out to matter, see the ancients, and post-1989 cohorts haven't had time to be canonized). Primary test: the top-1000 most eminent humans vs the full famous population, one-sided in the theory's favor. The theory survives only if (1) the primary test rejects in the predicted direction, (2) the real decade boundary is the most extreme of all 10 possible boundaries, and (3) the direction replicates in an independent fame metric.

Act II · First deathThe data said the theory was backwards

First result, within a day: the top-1000 all-time greats are less even-decade than the other 66,000 famous people. 514 of 1000 (51.4%) versus a pool share of 56.0%. The pre-registered pro-theory test fails completely (p = 0.999); read two-sided, the greats are significantly under-represented in even decades (p = 0.003). For a moment the data said greatness avoids even decades.

So… greatness avoids even decades? No. The pre-registered kill-shot caught the lie. There are exactly ten ways to slice years into alternating decade-parity groups: boundary at XX00 (the real decade), XX01, XX02, … XX09. A genuine decade effect must make the real boundary a discontinuous outlier. Instead:

 The naive gap slides smoothly across all ten offsets, the fingerprint of a birth-year trend, not a parity effect. After trend control, the real boundary ranks a thoroughly unremarkable 3rd of 10 and nothing approaches significance.

The trend was hiding in plain sight. All-time greats skew old (mean birth year 1870); the famous pool skews young (mean 1931), because Wikipedia is stuffed with athletes and actors born in the 1980s who will never be canonized. The 1980s is both the pool's largest decade (11,823 people) and an even one. Era was masquerading as parity.

Act III · ResurrectionThen the theory came back at z = +2.63

Fine: control for era. Compare each great only against famous people born in the same 20-year window, so every window holds one even and one odd decade. The result flipped hard: z = +2.63, p = 0.005. Confirmed! For about an hour the Even-Decade Theory was empirically alive.

Then one paranoid check. My 20-year windows started on even decades, so the even decade was always the older half of each window, and older means more canonized, even inside a 20-year block. Shift every window by exactly ten years and re-run the identical test: z = −2.74.

I now had a publishable p-value in each direction. Both were artifacts of one confound wearing two disguises.

The refereeA test neither disguise can fool

Aggregate everyone by birth year, then fit a regression: logit P(top-1000 | year) = smooth polynomial(year) + β·[even decade]. The polynomial absorbs the canonization trend at every scale; β can only pick up a signal that alternates decade-by-decade, which is exactly what the theory claims exists.

The oldest trapWhy the ancients can't testify

I wanted the great religious teachers and philosophers in the test: Newton (1643) and the ancients were part of the original hunch. But the recorded birth years of antiquity carry a fatal artifact: they are estimates rounded to round numbers, and a year ending in 0 always lands in an even decade.

 *Inflated by the window holding 15 even but 14 odd decades, a pre-registration arithmetic slip, disclosed in the decision log, which the internal baseline absorbs. The rounding gradient is the real story: ancient data fakes evidence FOR the theory mechanically, and the share of round years falls exactly as record-keeping improves.

Taking the recorded years at face value, the apex ancients still split: Muhammad (570), Aristotle, and Plato land odd-decade; Confucius and Luther even. But most ancient birth years (Moses, Abraham, the Buddha, Jesus) are tradition or scholarly estimates, not records. The honest position is that antiquity simply cannot testify either way.

Across fame intensity

Weighting every eligible person by their fame score shifts the even-decade share by −1.4 pp (bootstrap CI −1.5 to −1.3), the era artifact's direction, and it vanishes under trend control. Weighting by fame does not rescue the theory. Against the demographic births baseline, the naive top-1000 was null all along: 51.4% observed vs 52.6% expected (p = 0.47).

How the sausage was madeEvery judgment call, in order

The full log lives in DECISIONS.md; the short version:

  • Pre-registration locked. Groups, window, primary test, offset test, decision rule, all before any data. One arithmetic error included free of charge (the window holds 15 even, 14 odd decades), caught later and disclosed.
  • A newer dataset appeared mid-download. The 2025 Pantheon update was found in the same bucket. Kept 2020 as pre-registered primary; 2025 became the replication.
  • First run: theory backwards. The offset test's smooth gradient diagnosed a trend, not parity.
  • Era-matched fix: theory confirmed. z = +2.63. Then the alignment flip produced z = −2.74 and exposed the within-window gradient. Both results demoted from "evidence" to "exhibit."
  • GLM adopted as referee. Trend absorbed by a smooth curve; parity term isolated; checked at three polynomial degrees and all ten offsets.
  • User objection honored: test the apex. GOAT test, escalation curve, and the ancients, designed pro-theory, all null, with the one pro-theory blip (top-4, 2025, p = 0.046) disclosed rather than hidden.

What is real: an era effect. Greatness per birth cohort moves in long waves (the 1940s cohort of Ali, Marley, Merckx, and Spielberg; the 1980s football generation), and some legendary clusters happen to sit in even decades. A pattern-hungry mind promotes a cluster into a law, keeps the hits, and quietly drops Einstein, Babe Ruth, Kobe, and Brady.

The bigger lesson is the mirror: the same dataset handed me a significant result in both directions before it told the truth. If I had stopped at either point, I would have written a confident, chart-filled, wrong blog post. The pre-registered offset test saved me twice. The theory was fun. The autopsy was better.

Kick the tiresMethods, data, reproduction

The pipeline is one command from raw downloads to every figure on this page:

python run.py fetches Pantheon (2020 + 2025), Wikidata (SPARQL, chunked), the Nobel API, and OWID births/population (~85 MB, cached locally); cleans to parquet; runs the full battery (offset, permutation, era-matched both alignments, GLM at three degrees and ten offsets, per-field with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, Bayesian beta-binomial, fame-weighted bootstrap, temporal splits, GOAT/escalation/ ancients); and renders all nine figures. Every random draw is seeded, so every number here reproduces exactly. The cleaned parquets are committed, so you can skip the downloads and go straight to src/analysis.py.

HonestlyLimitations

Fame is not greatness. HPI and sitelinks measure Wikipedia-mediated memory, which is Western- and recency-skewed. But a parity law must show up in any reasonable eminence metric; it shows up in none of three. The pre-1950 births baseline approximates births as population × slowly-varying crude birth rate; it only affects the demographic-baseline tests, and parity contrasts are insensitive to smooth baseline error. The Wikidata replication is smaller than planned (5,126 vs a 15k–25k target; the sitelink floor of 60 was stricter than estimated). The era-matched test and the GLM were added after first results, disclosed in the decision log, and direction-symmetric: each could have rescued the theory exactly as easily as buried it; the pre-registered tests alone already refute. Nothing here speaks to post-1989 cohorts. The null's prediction: they will coin-flip too.
